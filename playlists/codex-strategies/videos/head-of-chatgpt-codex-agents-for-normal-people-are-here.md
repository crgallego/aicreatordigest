## Key Takeaway

> OpenAI’s head of ChatGPT and Codex says agents are finally reliable enough that non-technical knowledge workers will get the same leverage early adopters spent two years earning—within months, not years.

## In Context

Silicon Valley Girl interviews Tibo, who runs ChatGPT, Codex, and the APIs at OpenAI, on why agents are crossing from technical early adopters into ordinary knowledge work. The conversation is a live working session as much as a talk: they run Codex against email, calendar, computer use, and a half-built content app while Tibo explains what matured (long-horizon reliability, browser/computer use, 100+ plugins, auto-review safety) and what still needs packaging. It responds to the gap between software engineering’s AI transformation—Google’s 75% AI-written code is the reference point—and everyone else’s still-manual research, inbox, and ops load. Tibo’s through-line is augmentation with humans still on the hook, plus a near-term shift of agent memory and files into the cloud so the same assistant follows you across devices.

## Key Points

1. **Agents finally work for regular people** — Tibo’s claim is not that people will suddenly become technical; it’s that the stack matured. Agents are now reliable over long horizons, can use many tools including computer and browser use, and GPT-5.5-class models plus 100+ plugins make setup something non-technical users can actually finish.

   _My thought: Totally agree. Adoption is only accelerating, and you can tell by the types of tools that are being integrated now the direction they’re going is mass adoption._

2. **Knowledge work shifts in six months** — Silicon Valley Girl frames software engineering’s AI rewrite—Google at 75% AI-written code—as the preview. Tibo says that same transformation extends to all knowledge work over the next six months because reliability removed the need to babysit configs mid-run.

   _My thought: This is the main reason Idea Guys like me are Revengemaxxing._

3. **Marketer day compressed by schedules** — For a marketer, the unlock is naming the hour-blocks—market research, inbound email summaries, prospecting—and turning them into in-app schedules. What used to be an advanced cron concept is now “run this every 12 hours, do some market research, send me a PDF,” delivered by email or in-app.

   _My thought: Marketing is a great use-case example, but it’s expanding beyond the art department. Scheduled agentic workflows have found their way into my client’s construction operation within the first 30 days of starting._

4. **Auto review unlocks longer autonomy** — OpenAI shipped auto review: a main agent acts while a second agent verifies those actions for harm and low-risk behavior. It came out of safety and alignment research and is what makes longer autonomous runs on sensitive data acceptable—e.g. blocking an agent from emailing a stranger your personal info.

   _My thought: These built-in security checks are popping up everywhere, probably thanks to “vibe coders” like myself. And that’s a good thing._

5. **Local files give way to cloud memory** — Tibo still keeps a tidy local folder of notes and lets the agent organize it, but he says the next three months push this to the cloud so you’re not maintaining separate agents per laptop and phone. Memory and hosted files become the agent’s problem; he notes there’s still no great solution yet, though Google Drive folders work today.

   _My thought: So this was one of those huge fundamental changes in workflows that actually helped me understand how this stuff works on a deeper level. I was somewhat familiar with local software development, but the local folder workflow really unlocked system conceptualization._

6. **Tone of voice needs examples** — Must-have starter material for knowledge-worker agents includes tone of voice—but not as a written style guide. Tibo recommends past newsletters, recording snippets, and messages across professional and personal settings, plus per-project folders. Contacts and productivity apps can stay where they are; Codex can pull them.

   _My thought: Training agents on a set of writing samples seems far better than just instructions. One of those folders mentioned above should be References._

7. **Vibe coding still needs tech for scale** — Solo agent-built apps are fine for sharing with a few people or building for joy. If the goal is hundreds of thousands of users, a technical person in the mix is still useful for long-term structure. Tibo expects significant long-term maintainability gains in 6–9 months that reduce that dependency, but not overnight.

   _My thought: 💯_

8. **Humans stay on the hook** — Tibo rejects outsourcing responsibility: if you produce code and it breaks, it’s your fault, not the agent’s. Same for review. Agents augment and take boring work; humans remain in control and must still understand the system. Silicon Valley Girl’s tax and hiring dilemma gets the same answer—augment the person, don’t pretend the agent owns the outcome.

   _My thought: Maybe one day, then again maybe as AI gets better we can understand our value as humans better too._

9. **Half of Codex use is non-code** — Even among technical users, over 50% of tasks on Codex are already nontechnical. The app is about three months old (Codex CLI about a year); people use it to organize thinking, stay on unanswered email/Slack, strategize narrative order, and—with computer use—shop or pull analytics. Tibo calls their computer use the fastest implementation out there.

   _My thought: It’s a bit overwhelming at first, I agree. The latest update to the desktop app that splits Chat/Work with Codex is a step to help unify the ecosystem while also differentiating the products. Big fan._

10. **Prompt skill stops being the moat** — In 3–5 years Tibo expects dramatic change that reaches people who never actively prompt. Benefits stop being proportional to prompting craft and start looking like a tailor who already gets you—or ambient intelligence you engage as your authentic self in ordinary conversation.

   _My thought: I think we are all seeing the promptmaster era further and further in the rear view._


## Tactics

### Chief-of-staff daily brief _(workflow)_

Tag plugins (Gmail, calendar, docs) and prompt the agent to be your chief of staff: breakdown of the day, what matters, and prep. Run it at day start; power users make the prep request much more specific than the default. You can also ask where hours are wasted and whether to hire or build.

### Parallel agentic threads _(workflow)_

Spin multiple Codex threads at once instead of one chat: e.g. find podcast-related inbox threads and draft replies from memory/priorities; summarize what Codex shipped in two weeks; set Gmail filters; plan a trip from calendar availability; push content into Google Slides. Watch progress on the thread dashboard and stop any thread before it writes to real systems.

### Skill creator for daily loops _(workflow)_

Once a workflow works (example: collect LinkedIn analytics to a spreadsheet), use the skill creator—itself a skill—to capture it as a bespoke skill you can run every day. Pair with voice input if you prefer not to type the follow-ups.

### Tone and project file kit _(framework)_

Start agents with example-based tone-of-voice material (newsletters, recordings, messages) and a folder per project. Keep the library tidy enough for the agent to reorganize; don’t hand-maintain every contact in files if Codex can read your existing productivity apps.

### Auto review before deep access _(guardrail)_

Treat dual-agent auto review as the prerequisite for giving an agent broad life access. Main agent acts; verifier blocks harmful or high-risk steps so longer autonomous runs—including on sensitive data—stay inside a risk box you’re willing to accept.
