## Key Takeaway

> A company OS works by encoding the 1% AI-pilled operators' skills into shared GitHub skill files and surfacing them just-in-time in Slack so the other 90–99% know what to use when.

## In Context

Aakash Gupta sits down with Jiaona Zhang (Jay-Z), CPO at Laurel and PM instructor at Stanford, formerly CPO at Linktree and a product leader at Airbnb, Webflow, Dropbox, and WeWork. She screen-shares Laurel's full company operating system: a GitHub repo organized by every function, with skill files for each repeatable activity, loaded into Claude and delivered through daily briefings and Slack. The conversation is a practical build guide for teams stuck between a few highly AI-pilled ICs and an org that does not know what to automate. Zhang walks through how to start from one tedious workflow, graduate to playbooks and agents, run non-engineers shipping front-end and back-end features with Devin, and hire for AI maturity by making candidates screen-share how they actually work.

## Key Points

1. **The 1% vs 99% AI gap** — Zhang's core org problem is not tool access. A small set of highly AI-pilled users tinker with workflows while 90–99% of the company is unsure what to use when. The company OS exists to take what the 1% designs and spread it so adoption is consistent across functions, not lumpy by individual.
2. **GitHub as the company OS** — Laurel keeps a company-wide OS in GitHub with folders for every function—customer success, data science, design, engineering, finance, legal, marketing, product—and nested activities under each. Every activity folder holds skill files that define how to run that motion (renewals, handoffs, session prep, negotiation support) so Claude can call the right skill in context.
3. **Ontology maps work to automate** — Every function's work is mapped to an ontology of categories and tasks. That map decides which skills to build, which work should go up in time spent (feature work, QA, shipping), and which tedious work (competitive synthesis, detailed briefs, research logistics) should be agent-called or removed. For product, the work map is intentionally starting to look like an engineer's.
4. **Four levels of AI maturity** — Zhang assesses people and orgs on four levels: (1) chat mode with ChatGPT/Claude like search, (2) automating a workflow, (3) building apps for tedious work, (4) building shared apps and truly shipping to customers. Screen-sharing how someone actually works quickly reveals whether they are level one or further along.
5. **PMs ship front-end and back-end** — At Laurel, PMs ship real features end-to-end with Devin—not only button-copy experiments. Examples include temporary initiatives (front-end plus back-end, tied into PMS systems) and empty states for new-user onboarding, owned by PMs who did not start as engineers. Customer success has also shipped using the same enablement guide.
6. **Captains model replaces rigid roles** — Every initiative gets a captain whose skill set is the bottleneck: engineering for architectural change, design when interaction is king, PM when deep customer and business context (and content) is the hard part. LLMs and code tools help non-captains pressure-test risk; engineers still code-review the contentious pieces.
7. **Two tracks for product review** — Small features compress to a day or an hour: captain owns end-to-end quality, visibility in an ask-Devin channel, PR review, no full product review. Large systemic changes (e.g. how activities are displayed across the product) still get product strategy and architectural review. Temporary initiatives did not go through product review. Zhang rejects the idea that AI-native means roadmaps and planning disappear.
8. **AI ops as the new biz ops** — Making AI everyone's job often makes it no one's. Laurel staffed a dedicated AI operations team—starting with one person (Sasha) who proved value—then expanded so functions want their own AI ops person for GTM, product, finance. Hire for extreme curiosity, tooling tinkering, and relentless efficiency-finding.
9. **Lean senior IC product orgs** — Zhang has run orgs of hundreds; she now has five PMs and four designers. More people add coordination cost and dilute end-to-end ownership. She hires battle-tested seniors who are excited and hands-on—including former CPOs/VPs who prefer building over managing—and pays fewer seasoned people more rather than staffing an army.
10. **Fundamentals hold, tools changed** — Teaching PM 101 and AI leadership (Stanford, Yale, Reforge) has convinced her the core principles never changed and matter more: do not jump to solution, know why and for whom, define success. What changed is speed, bureaucracy-blasting, culture, OS, and how leaders empower teams. Reforge AI leadership curriculum shifts by the month; fundamentals do not.

## Tactics

### Three steps to a company OS _(framework)_

Start small: pick one tedious, repeatable workflow (feature-request triage in Slack, CRM data entry, templated email) and automate the intake, assignment, and ticket. Next, write the full playbook (Laurel's CS playbook ran ~50–55 pages; first drafts with Claude take minutes to hours), audit human-only steps vs agent/productizable steps, and turn steps into skills. Then wrap sub-agents in a mega agent (e.g. a go-to-market agent) that routes asks, and deliver just-in-time inside Slack/email rather than a separate agent UI.

### Skills in Claude over scheduled overload _(workflow)_

Encode the OS as skill files and upload them into company Claude context so people invoke skills like /morning briefing product in the flow of work. Zhang found personal scheduled automations became overkill and added information overload; Laurel consolidated into one daily briefing surface and just-in-time delivery so the least AI-proficient staff still hit the same bar.

### Ask Devin reviewers and ground rules _(guardrail)_

Transparency first: a shared channel for Devin usage where the right FE engineer or designer gets tagged. Enablement guides set ground rules; support or CS ideas get a quick check and a first version to play with before heavier process. That compresses what used to be a scheduled multi-stakeholder product review while keeping risky code under engineer review.

### Screen-share AI interview _(framework)_

For every function, not only PM, Zhang has candidates screen-share how they use AI. Talk is cheap at level one; live screen reveals workflow automations, personal apps, shared apps, and real shipping. Prefer a few seasoned, AI-pilled orchestrators over many people who need constant designer/engineer complement.

### Systematize unreasonable hospitality _(workflow)_

Codify cultural values into the OS instead of a forgotten doc. For customer check-ins, skills pull Gong context and suggest surprise-and-delight moves (e.g. engraved passport holder) with logistics handled so humans stay on relationship work agents cannot replace—onsites, dinners, real hospitality—while scheduling and prep get automated.

I mean, I’m sure organizations and tech forward places like Silicon Valley have probably been using that term more broadly for some time so it may be nothing new there.
